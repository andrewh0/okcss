On Leaving Things Alone

October 17, 2026

For most of my career I measured progress by what I added. A new feature, a new page, a new setting someone had asked for. It felt like momentum. Looking back, a surprising amount of my best work was deciding not to do something, and then defending that decision long enough for it to matter.

I've been thinking about this more as the tools get faster. When making a thing costs almost nothing, the difficult part moves somewhere else. It moves to choosing. Anyone can produce ten versions of an idea before lunch now. Fewer people can tell you which nine to throw away, or why.

Restraint is hard to show in a portfolio. Nobody screenshots the button that isn't there. But you can feel it in the things you return to: a notebook with good paper, a website that loads before you've finished blinking, an app that hasn't changed its icon in a decade.

So what does it actually take to leave something alone?

Know what the thing is for

You can't remove anything until you know what the work is supposed to do. Most clutter comes from never answering that question, so every request seems equally reasonable. Once the purpose is clear, a lot of decisions make themselves. The feature that doesn't serve it can wait, probably forever.1

This sounds obvious. It isn't. Writing the purpose down in one sentence is uncomfortable, because it closes doors.

Let defaults do the work

The best choices are the ones people never notice. A sensible line length, enough space between paragraphs, text dark enough to read without effort. None of these will win an award. All of them decide whether someone finishes reading.

Good defaults are a kind of generosity. They assume the person on the other end has better things to think about than your settings page.

Leave room to be wrong

Doing less also means there's less to undo. Every addition is a small promise to maintain it, explain it and keep it working. When I'm unsure, I've started to wait. If the need is real, it will come back, usually clearer than before, and often with a simpler answer attached.2

None of this is new. It's just easy to forget when everything around you is getting faster.

  1. I keep a short list of things we decided not to build, with a sentence on why. It has saved more arguments than any roadmap.
  2. The simpler answer is rarely the first one. It tends to show up once the urgency has worn off.
